Can composition features help identify known superconductors whose critical temperature exceeds the liquid-nitrogen threshold?

What we corrected

An eLCS iteration presents one training record, not an epoch. Our original selected model received only 1,000 updates: about 5.9% of its training records received direct rule-learning updates. We initially gave this limitation insufficient prominence.

All supplied training records still informed feature characterization and class counts. The error was the limited rule-learning budget and its interpretation, not fabricated scores.

We tested six single-model configurations: 1, 5 and 20 final-development pass equivalents, each at population limits 100 and 1,000. Selection used mean development balanced accuracy, with ties preferring fewer updates, then a smaller population.

The selected single model and each ensemble member received $iterations updates over $training_rows training records: $passes complete passes, with population limit $population.

The same fixed update budget gave approximately $cv_passes passes in the smaller CV training partitions of $cv_training_min to $cv_training_max records. Training order was shuffled once per fit, not reshuffled at each pass.

Twenty passes do not establish convergence or best achievable performance. Both budget and population changed relative to the original selected model; gains cannot be attributed to longer training alone.

How do these findings compare with the original?

Original evaluation balanced accuracy was 63.15% for single eLCS and 58.32% for the ensemble. The revision reached $single_score and $ensemble_score respectively. The single model's gain was modest; the ensemble reversed its earlier disadvantage.

The selected single model's mean development balanced accuracy rose from 61.9% to $single_cv_score. This is not the same quantity as evaluation balanced accuracy.

We deliberately reused the dataset and evaluation records after discovering the mistakes. The results are exploratory; greater training exposure cannot undo prior data exposure.

Read the scores without the jargon

All metrics below use reused evaluation records, not training results. Precision, recall and F1 treat above 77 K as the positive class.

What does each metric mean?
Balanced accuracy
The average of recall for the two classes. Each class has equal importance, even though above-77-K records are less common. The primary comparison metric.
Precision
Of records predicted to be above 77 K, how many actually were? Higher precision means fewer false leads.
Recall
Of records actually above 77 K, how many did the model find? Higher recall means fewer missed candidates.
F1
The harmonic mean of positive-class precision and recall. It balances these two quantities but does not include true negatives.

Comparative conclusions

The recorded results support composition-based classification within this dataset, but not a claim of independent materials discovery. The useful comparison is not simply which model has the largest score: it is what each model finds, misses and costs.

Random Forest offered the strongest balance

Its 90.4% balanced accuracy and 84.3% F1 led the five evaluated systems. It detected 656 of 779 positive records, missed 123 and produced 121 false positives. That is substantially broader candidate coverage than either revised eLCS system.

This supports Random Forest as the strongest recorded reference, not as a universally superior algorithm. The conventional models used fixed project defaults, and neither equal-compute tuning nor significance tests between them were performed.

Precision is not candidate coverage

The revised ensemble had the highest positive precision, $ensemble_precision, versus 84.4% for Random Forest. But it detected only $ensemble_detected positive records and missed $ensemble_missed. Fewer false leads came with many more missed candidates.

High precision alone therefore does not make eLCS the best screening system. Broad discovery favors recall; a small, selective shortlist emphasizes precision. The current evidence does not include threshold tuning or experimentally validated recommendations.

The simpler conventional references remained competitive

Logistic regression reached 80.8% balanced accuracy, compared with SVM's 79.0%; their positive recalls were 68.8% and 65.1%. Both were ahead of the revised eLCS pair on balanced accuracy, recall and F1, though below Random Forest.

Logistic regression's descriptive lead does not establish statistical superiority, and the results do not prove that more complex models always perform better. They compare these recorded settings and preprocessing choices.

Development and evaluation tell different stories

Five-fold development balanced accuracy favored single eLCS: $single_cv_score versus $ensemble_cv_score. On reused evaluation records, the ensemble instead led by $difference points. The ensemble advantage was small and did not persist across both stages, despite three times the aggregate record-update budget.

The conventional development ranking matched evaluation: Random Forest 91.2%, logistic regression 80.8%, SVM 78.6%. These are unweighted fold means, not independent replications or confidence intervals. Inspect all development scores and fold standard deviations.

Historical comparisons: repair, deduplication and retraining

Library correctness mattered. In the original matched 1,000-update evaluation, unmodified deduplicated eLCS achieved 47.5% balanced accuracy; the corrected version achieved 63.1%. The majority-class fallback repair was the only difference in that pair. This is distinct from the later training-budget revision.

Deduplication was a separate data-handling choice. At the original 1,000-update / population-100 setting, corrected development balanced accuracy was 59.5% with raw training rows and 61.9% with exact training-row deduplication. That descriptive difference is not proof that deduplication always helps. Different continuous-temperature measurements were retained, and evaluation rows were never removed.

The original ensemble result was unfavorable. It reached 58.32% evaluation balanced accuracy versus 63.15% for single eLCS. The original group-paired statistical comparison favored the single model under its stated assumptions; it does not apply to the retrained pair. The original ensemble's higher ordinary accuracy did not overturn its loss on the primary metric.

Retraining changed the result, not the evidence boundary. Single eLCS rose by approximately 0.80 percentage points and the ensemble by approximately 6.18 points on reused evaluation records. Both budget and population increased, so longer training alone cannot explain these gains. Twenty passes still did not close the gap to the conventional references or demonstrate convergence.

Sources: original results and statistical assumptions, original matched development findings, and completed exploratory revision. Historical scores are not additional models in the current five-model chart.

What we can take forward

Keep balanced accuracy and positive recall visible alongside precision; treat readable rules as inspectable patterns, not validated physical explanations; and separate correctness repairs from modeling enhancements. An independent dataset with material-family-aware separation, a justified training/convergence study and experimentally checked candidates would be needed to strengthen the claims. These are next steps, not completed validations.

What the learned rules say

These are three real rules from the retrained ensemble, one per seed. Each rule describes a combination of feature conditions and votes for one class.

Within a member, all matching rules vote using fitness multiplied by numerosity. The ensemble then takes the majority of the three member predictions. A single sample rule does not determine the final answer.

If the rules cannot produce a useful decision, the corrected library falls back to the majority training class. That fallback is not a rule-based explanation.

Training matches are repeated learning experiences, not independent validation. These rules are inspectable model patterns, not physical laws or trustworthy standalone recommendations.

$rules

Within each seed, the example with most training matches for the illustrated class was selected; fitness breaks ties. Selection did not use evaluation success. “Weighted” features account for elemental proportions; valence, mass and radius are aggregate composition descriptors. Full, unrounded conditions are in the linked CSVs.

Where the evidence stops

These findings apply to known superconductors in this dataset. A prediction does not establish that an arbitrary material is superconducting or commercially useful.

  • The evaluation records appeared in earlier full-data analyses and rule exports, and their original scores were viewed before this revision. They are not an untouched external dataset.
  • Identical feature vectors were grouped together, but related compositions and material families may still cross partitions.
  • A bounded budget grid was tested. The largest budget won development selection, but convergence was not established and the grid was not extended after revised evaluation scores.
  • The library correctness repair, increased training exposure and ensemble are separate changes. The ensemble's small revised advantage is descriptive, and conventional models remain fixed references rather than tuned, equal-compute competitors.
  • Model predictions are screening signals. Physical feasibility, operating margin, current, field response, cost and experimental validation remain outside this evaluation.
